Overview

Foundation Studio II is a studio about movement and choreography: how a body moves from A to B, and how landscape design shapes that passage. The semester is divided evenly into two projects that share one conceptual spine. Each project has a dominant axis of movement, not an exclusive one.

Project 1, Move Vertically (Z), is set in Amelia Gorman Park, a very steep hill rising from Broadway to Wadsworth Terrace between West 189th and West 190th Streets. Students design and choreograph a vertical movement through landform and tectonics. How do you spend a rise?

Project 2, Move Horizontally (XY), is set along St. Nicholas Avenue from 110th to 125th Street. Students design and choreograph movement along a corridor, across a plane, through sequence, rhythm and threshold. Landform from Project 1 carries over as a secondary thread. How do you score a duration?

Both projects share one vocabulary. A path is the line a body takes from A to B. A pause is where the body stops: a landing, a seat, a ledge. A threshold marks a change of condition, in ground, light, enclosure or sound. A view is what the body sees as it moves, and what it is made to wait for. Effort is what the body spends, in rise climbed and distance walked. Tempo is how fast the body moves and how that pace changes along the way. Choreography is the arrangement of these terms in time, and the studio treats landform, structure, planting and paving as the instruments that write it.

The outcome of each project is NOT a park plan seen from above. Rather, it is a movement designed from the body: drawn in section and sequence and tested in model. Plans still matter, but they serve the passage they describe.

Methodology

The studio runs as two sections of 9 to 10 students. Both instructors teach, lecture and run workshops in both projects. Sections swap instructors at the start of Project 2, so every student works with both.

Project 1 closes with a midterm science fair, where both sections hang their work in one room and external guests circulate. Project 2 closes with a science-fair pin-up followed by a final review with guest critics. The midterm and the final carry equal weight.

Studio time is mostly desk critiques, where each student reviews work in progress with an instructor. Lectures introduce the ideas and precedents behind each phase. Workshops teach a technique alongside the project, such as lofting landform in Grasshopper, modeling landform in Rhino, fabricating inserts and mapping movement. Each project opens with a site visit. Amelia Gorman Park itself is not accessible, so Project 1 reads it from its edges on Broadway and Wadsworth Terrace, from the base data and from the site model. St. Nicholas Avenue is open to the body, so Project 2 is walked, timed and recorded on site before it is drawn. Pin-ups between reviews let students see each other's work and practice presenting it.

Design is an iterative process. Students work on models and drawings at the same time, and revise both after every critique.

The studio is built from small studies of about one week each, so every student makes something new every week. A study is assigned on Monday, checked in on Thursday and due the following Monday, which leaves the weekend to work on it. Each study is an inquiry into one question, and each one starts from the work of the last.

Students are graded on this run of studies, ten in all at 9% each, each assessed when it is due, rather than on a final push. Study 1.0 is an ungraded warm-up. Work saved for the last week, however polished its renderings, cannot make up for studies that were missed along the way. Each study has its own page on this site, listed in the side panel, with its brief, learning objectives and expected outcome. This site is the course’s single source for every brief and due date.

Course Logistics / Technology

Site model (P1). Each section builds one of two identical chipboard pancake site models of Amelia Gorman Park from a base file the instructors provide, with the existing topography of the design zone cut out as a void. Students design their own landform as inserts that plug into the void. Inserts may be 3D printed, hand-built or laser-cut, and must fit either model. The model is 30" × 30" at 1" = 20', covering 600 ft: the park and about half a block around it. Each 0.05" chipboard layer is one 1 ft contour, so the vertical scale is true and the model stands about 6" tall. The laser bed is smaller than the model, so each layer is cut in two halves and joined on one straight seam. The void is an 8" × 8" square on the slope, aligned to the model frame. Its floor is the top of the base board, and every insert sits flush on it. Hand-built inserts use oil-based plasticine, which is reusable and keeps its size: fill the core with scrap, foam or chipboard and sculpt only the top half inch in clay.

P2. No physical model is required. Small hand-built study models are welcome.

Software. Rhino 3D with Grasshopper is the main tool, for landform, inserts and fabrication files. AutoCAD and Adobe Creative Cloud (Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign) carry the drawings and layouts. All are on the computers in the SSA labs. Students who work on personal machines obtain their own licenses, many free or at reduced cost through CCNY: Rhino at rhino3d.com/educational, AutoCAD at autodesk.com/education, and Adobe through CUNY Login.

Materials and costs. The studio materials fund covers the chipboard, glue and plasticine for both site models, which are already purchased for the class. Glue layers with beads and dots rather than a full spread, which warps chipboard and uses three times the glue. 3D printing and laser cutting go through the SSA fabrication facilities, and students cover their own material. Printing for pin-ups and reviews is the student's cost. Both sites are in Manhattan and reached by subway, so each site visit costs a fare each way.

Personal Laptop. Students are strongly encouraged to bring a personal laptop to each class session, capable of running Rhino 3D and Adobe Creative Cloud.

CCNY Email. Communication will happen via CCNY Email. Students need to check and be responsive to their CCNY emails.

Support of Program Mission

This course is part of the Landscape Architecture course sequence and introduces the disciplinary skills and thinking processes necessary for a student preparing for a career as a professional landscape architect. The course supports the mission of the Graduate Program in Landscape Architecture at the Bernard and Anne Spitzer School of Architecture of the City College of New York, which asserts that landscape architecture plays an essential role in connecting social justice to environmental design and the planning of landscape systems in the urban realm. The Graduate Program prepares students to be leaders in the field through innovative research and practice in urban ecological design, planning, and policymaking. This course teaches students responsive technologies and strategies to address issues of globalization, expanding urbanization, social and environmental justice, the transformation of land management practices in response to diminishing natural resources, and resilient adaptation to climate change.

Educational Goals
  • Site Reading through the Body
  • Landform, Tectonics and Fabrication
  • Design through Movement, Sequence and Choreography
  • Schematic Design and Visual Communication
Learning Objectives

One area for each educational goal above. By the end of the semester, students will be able to:

  • Site Reading — Walk a site and record it with the body, then translate that record into maps, sections and diagrams that hold the site's history, culture, use and ground.
  • Landform and Tectonics — Read and draw contours, calculate slope and grade, and design landform and structure (stairs, ramps, walls, terraces) that resolve a 110 ft rise at a known scale, in Rhino and in physical model.
  • Choreography — Describe and design a movement as a sequence of paths, pauses, thresholds and views, using a notation of their own invention, serial views and timed sections, and argue for the experience it produces.
  • Visual Communication and Critical Thinking — Develop and recursively revise drawings, models and verbal presentations into a schematic design proposal for an urban landscape, with clear visual arguments and a consistent graphic structure.
Assignments

Each project is a sequence of weekly studies; the linked study pages hold each brief. An asterisk (*) marks a required item.

Project 1, Move Vertically. Students design a vertical movement through Amelia Gorman Park, from Broadway up to Wadsworth Terrace, a rise of about 110 ft. The question is how a body spends that rise: where it climbs hard, where it rests, and what it sees on the way.

  • *1.0 Terms: ungraded warm-up, three 4″ × 4″ models of three landform terms, from found material
  • *1.1 Catalog: twelve Grasshopper isometrics on one 11×17 sheet, four per term
  • *1.2 Clay: three clay iterations of one catalog item
  • *1.3 Occupy: plan enlargements and sections at 1″ = 8′, and a revised model
  • *1.4 Scale: an insert fitted to the site-model void in clay at 1″ = 20′
  • *1.5 Proposal: a contour plan and long section at 1″ = 20′ and a perspective section collage, at the midterm science fair (S14)
  • *Section site model, built together by each section (S04 to S06)

Project 2, Move Horizontally. Students design a movement along St. Nicholas Avenue from 110th to 125th Street, fifteen blocks of corridor. The question is how to score a duration: how sequence, rhythm and threshold turn a long walk into a passage with a structure. Landform from Project 1 carries over as a secondary thread.

The project opens with a mapping exercise. Each student invents a score, a notation of their own, to document how bodies move along the avenue: who walks, waits, crosses and lingers, at what pace and where. Precedents for notation range from serial views to dance scores, and students study several before inventing their own. The same score is then used to write the proposed movement, so the design is drawn in the language the site was read in.

  • *Movement mapping: an invented score and the maps it produces from the site visit and timed walks
  • *A corridor plan of the fifteen blocks that shows the sequence as a whole
  • *Zoomed-in plans and sections of key moments, such as crossings, pauses and thresholds
  • *A score of the proposed movement, written in the same notation, showing sequence, rhythm and tempo
  • *Experiential drawings, including serial views along the path
  • *At least one section that carries the Project 1 landform thread into the corridor
  • Optional study models, sketches and animations
Guest Critics

Each project closes with a formal review with guest critics: the midterm science fair at S14 (Mon 3/22) for Project 1, where external guests circulate among both sections’ work, and the final review at S28 (Thu 5/20) for Project 2. At these reviews, students present their propositions visually and verbally to open a discussion of the ideas in their work. Critics will be announced before each review.

Grading Criteria

All students are held to high and consistent standards. Work is considered on its own merits and in relationship to the work of others, to make clear distinctions across the range of work. The full range of the grading scale may be used. Grades are not negotiable. Design work is graded on the following criteria:

  • Completeness — Assignments are complete and graphically legible.
  • Technical Skills — Executed with appropriate method and proficiency across media.
  • Accuracy and Presentation — Completed with precision and presented professionally.
  • Effort and Concept — Iterates through multiple drafts; shows experimentation and improvement; concepts are articulated and well-developed.
  • Participation — Engages in discussions and group work.
  • Attendance — Consistent preparation and on-time presence.

Academic Integrity

Academic dishonesty is prohibited at CUNY and is punishable by penalties including failing grades, suspension, and expulsion. Plagiarism — presenting the words, drawings, ideas, or opinions of someone else as one's own — is a serious instance of academic dishonesty. All assignments must be the student's original work, produced for this class and no other. Any copying, even short excerpts, from another book, article, or Internet source, published or unpublished, without proper attribution or citation, will result in automatic failure of the course.

You will fail this course if you: (1) submit work used for another course; (2) copy material and submit it as your own without quotation and citation, or otherwise represent another's work as your own; (3) submit the same work as another student. Plagiarized work will be reported to the CCNY Academic Integrity Committee per the CUNY Policy on Academic Integrity.

CCNY Academic Integrity Policy

Incompletes

Grades of "incomplete" (INC) are not given unless there is evidence of a medical or personal emergency. In such cases, instructor and student develop a contract to complete work by a specified date, per CCNY policy. This contract must be processed by the academic advisor and approved by the Program Director and the Chair's Office. Missed work due to illness must be explained with a physician's note.

Classroom Civility

All students are expected to participate in class discussions. Meaningful and constructive dialogue is encouraged; discussion should be civil, respectful, and relevant to the topic. This requires a willingness to listen, tolerance for different points of view, and mutual respect from all participants. Guest critics will be informed of this expectation for civility.

Use of Electronic Devices

Students are expected to engage in active learning during class time. Mobile phones, texting, and social media are distracting; please put handheld devices away and out of view during class. The appropriate use of laptops will be discussed. The instructor provides periodic breaks; personal devices may be used during these.
